Understanding Health Insurance

Health insurance is a contract where you pay monthly premiums to an insurance company, and in return, they cover a portion or all of your medical expenses. The system pools money from all policyholders to pay for healthcare costs when members need medical care.

Your plan includes a deductible (what you pay before coverage starts), copayments (fixed amounts per service), and an out-of-pocket maximum (the most you'll pay annually). Health insurance protects you from catastrophic medical costs while providing access to preventive care and medical services at negotiated rates — helping prevent financial hardship from unexpected illness or injury.

Plan types vary. HMOs require you to choose a primary care physician and get referrals. PPOs give you more flexibility to see specialists without referrals. EPOs combine elements of both. Understanding which type suits your situation is one of the most important decisions you'll make during open enrollment.

💰

Premium

The monthly amount you pay to keep your health insurance active, regardless of whether you use medical services that month.

🎯

Deductible

The amount you must pay out-of-pocket for covered services before your insurance company starts sharing the cost.

🤝

Copayment

A fixed amount you pay for a covered health service (e.g., $30 for a doctor visit), usually after your deductible is met.

🛡️

Out-of-Pocket Maximum

The most you will have to pay for covered services in a plan year. After you reach this limit, insurance pays 100%.

📋

Network

The group of doctors, hospitals, and other providers your insurance company has contracted with at negotiated rates.

📅

Open Enrollment

The annual period when you can sign up for, change, or drop health insurance coverage. Missing it may limit your options.

🏥

ACA / Marketplace

The Affordable Care Act created Health Insurance Marketplaces where individuals can compare and buy subsidized plans.

💊

Formulary

Your plan's list of covered prescription drugs. Drugs are grouped into tiers that determine your cost-sharing amount.

Affordable Care Act

The Affordable Care Act (ACA) Explained

The Affordable Care Act — also known as the ACA or "Obamacare" — is a federal law signed in 2010 that transformed how Americans access and pay for health insurance. Its core goal is to make affordable, comprehensive health coverage available to everyone, regardless of income or pre-existing medical conditions.

Under the ACA, insurers cannot deny coverage or charge higher premiums based on health status. All marketplace plans must cover 10 essential health benefits, including emergency services, prescription drugs, preventive care, maternity care, and mental health services.

One of the most impactful provisions of the ACA is the Premium Tax Credit (subsidy) — financial assistance available to individuals and families earning between 100% and 400% of the federal poverty level. Enhanced subsidies introduced in 2021 have expanded eligibility further, making many plans available for as little as $0/month for qualifying households.

🚫 No Denials for Pre-Existing Conditions

Insurers cannot refuse to cover you or charge you more because of medical history, chronic illness, or a previous diagnosis.

💵 Premium Tax Credits (Subsidies)

Depending on your income, you may qualify for substantial financial help that lowers your monthly premium — sometimes to $0.

✅ 10 Essential Health Benefits

All ACA-compliant plans cover emergency care, hospitalization, maternity, mental health, prescriptions, preventive services, and more.

📅 Open Enrollment & Special Enrollment

The standard ACA open enrollment runs November 1 – January 15 in Florida. Life events like job loss or marriage trigger Special Enrollment Periods.

👨‍👩‍👧 Children Covered to Age 26

The ACA requires insurers to allow parents to keep adult children on their health plan through age 26, regardless of student or marital status.

🔒 Annual & Lifetime Limit Protections

Insurers cannot impose dollar limits on covered essential benefits, protecting you from having your benefits cut off mid-treatment.

Vision Insurance

Vision Insurance: What You Need to Know

Vision insurance is a separate type of coverage designed to reduce the cost of routine eye care — including eye exams, prescription glasses, and contact lenses. Most standard health insurance plans do not cover routine vision care for adults, making a standalone vision plan a smart addition to your overall coverage strategy.

Vision plans typically work on a benefit schedule: you receive an annual allowance for frames or contacts, and exams are covered (sometimes with a small copay). Plans differ in which eye care providers are in-network, so it's important to verify that your preferred optometrist or ophthalmologist participates before you enroll.

🔍

Eye Exams

Most vision plans cover one comprehensive eye exam per year at little or no cost. Regular exams detect early signs of conditions like glaucoma, macular degeneration, and diabetes.

👓

Eyeglasses & Frames

Plans provide an annual frame allowance (commonly $100–$200) plus coverage for prescription lenses. Upgrades like anti-reflective coating or progressive lenses may cost extra.

🥽

Contact Lenses

If you prefer contacts over glasses, most plans offer an annual contact lens benefit in lieu of the eyeglass allowance — typically $100–$150 toward contacts and a fitting exam.

💡

LASIK & Refractive Surgery

Some vision plans offer discounts on LASIK or other refractive surgeries through participating providers, even though the procedure itself is generally not fully covered.

👨‍👩‍👧

Family Coverage

Adding vision coverage for your family is often very affordable. Children's vision care is an essential health benefit under the ACA and is included in all marketplace plans.

💳

Standalone vs. Bundle

You can purchase a standalone vision plan or bundle it with dental coverage for a lower combined premium. Our advisors compare options across carriers to find the best value for you.
